Marketing analytics / Research case study

How last-touch shapes channel performance.

Before an order is placed, a customer may interact with several marketing channels. Attribution is the rule used to assign those channels credit for the order. Last-touch attribution assigns all that credit to the final eligible interaction.

That final-interaction rule was my starting concern. I wanted to investigate how the assessment of channel performance changes when order credit is reported under different attribution models and lookback windows.

Research question

How does using last-touch attribution shape the assessment of marketing channel performance, and how does that assessment change under alternative models and lookback windows?

Last-touch provides the baseline. Linear and algorithmic attribution provide two alternative allocations, each reported with a 14-day and a 30-day lookback.

The analysis examines differences in reported order credit, channel rankings, and the consistency of those differences across settings.

16 marketing channels3 attribution models2 lookback windows1 reporting month

Analytics tools make these allocation choices part of reporting. Their defaults vary: Google Analytics 4 defaults to data-driven attribution for event-scoped key-event reporting; CJA allows attribution settings at the metric and report level. This study explicitly uses last-touch as its comparison baseline.

Main findings

The reported performance picture changes in three ways.

In these demo exports, changing attribution settings alters more than the amount of order credit. It can change the direction of a comparison and the label attached to a channel, while leaving its relative rank unchanged.

The following three chapters explain how the comparisons were constructed, examine the evidence behind these findings, and interpret what they suggest for assessing channel performance.

Chapter 01 / Study design

How the comparison was set up.

To examine the influence of the final-interaction rule, I compared last-touch with two alternatives. The attribution model determines how credit is allocated; the lookback window determines which preceding interactions are eligible.

Last-touch

All credit goes to the most recent eligible touchpoint before the order.

Linear

Credit is shared equally across eligible touchpoints.

Algorithmic

Adobe uses a statistical allocation method to distribute credit across eligible interactions. This study compares the exported allocations.

The data and comparison scope

The analysis uses four channel-level exports from Adobe CJA’s Omni-Channel - Multi-Industry demo. The reporting period is May 1–31, 2026, and every attribution setting uses the Person container, which considers interactions at person scope rather than within a single session.

A 14-day or 30-day lookback is measured backwards from each order. It is an eligibility window, rather than a different reporting month. All 16 channels are retained, including digital advertising, messaging, search, and offline channels, to preserve the mix across which credit is allocated.

The exports already contain the three model outputs. I cleaned and joined them by channel using Python, then calculated relative credit changes and order-volume ranks. This is a descriptive comparison of exported allocations.

For each channel, relative credit change is calculated as 100 × (alternative orders − last-touch orders) / last-touch orders, using the same window for both inputs. A +20% result therefore means 1.2 times the baseline credit. Calculations retain full precision; displayed percentages are rounded to two decimal places.

Data preparation, validation, and source files

Preparation

  1. Read the four CSV exports after their metadata headers.
  2. Remove exported totals and breakdown-label rows, and trim channel names.
  3. Join on marketing channel, keeping the attribution export as the 16-channel base.
  4. Calculate comparisons from full-precision values. Round only displayed results.

Checks

Calculation definitions and glossary

Relative credit change (%)

100 × (alternative orders − last-touch orders) / last-touch orders

Both inputs use the same window. A +20% value means the alternative model gives 1.2 times the last-touch order credit.

Order-volume rank

Order channels from most to least attributed orders separately for each model and window. A positive rank change means moving closer to rank 1.

Conversion environment share (%)

100 × environment orders / sum of the channel’s five environment counts

This denominator equals the channel’s 30-day last-touch attributed orders.

Revenue per attributed order

reported total revenue / 30-day last-touch attributed orders

The export does not identify the currency or fully document the revenue metric’s configuration.

Correlation

Pearson uses the full-precision Lifecycle Index and algorithmic relative credit change. Spearman uses their average ranks. Both include all 16 channels and the 30-day attribution comparison.

Attributed orders
Order credit assigned to a channel by a specified attribution setting. Fractional credit can produce decimal values.
Percentage change
A relative difference divided by the last-touch baseline, expressed out of 100.
Percentage points (pp)
The arithmetic difference between two percentages. A change from 20% to 30% is 10 percentage points.
Container
The scope used to group interactions for attribution. This export uses Person.
Conversion environment
The order-completion setting: Mobile App, Website, Mobile Web, Point of Sale, or Call Center.
Correlation
A descriptive measure of association. Its value does not establish cause and effect.

With the rules and denominator established, the next comparison asks how much the same channel’s reported credit changes, and whether that also changes its position in the channel mix.

Chapter 02 / Results

What changes when the attribution settings change?

The model comparison addresses the first finding: a channel’s assessment depends on the allocation rule. Rankings and lookback comparisons then show which aspects of that assessment remain consistent.

The model changes the allocation, sometimes in opposite directions.

At 30 days, algorithmic attribution gives Paid Search 12.09% more order credit than last-touch, while linear gives 5.71% less. The same directional disagreement appears at 14 days. For this channel, the interpretation changes according to which alternative is selected.

Other channels show a recurring direction. Direct Mail receives more credit under both alternatives at both windows. At 30 days, its increases are 182.78% under algorithmic attribution and 149.05% under linear. The chart places these examples within the full 16-channel comparison.

A change in credit does not necessarily change channel rank.

Email illustrates the second finding. Its algorithmic order credit is 43.90% lower than last-touch at 30 days, whereas linear gives it 2.35% more. Despite those differences, Email remains first by attributed orders in all six settings.

The reported order totals also differ across windows.

The exports total 231,678 orders at 14 days and 236,183 at 30 days, an increase of 4,505 or 1.94% relative to the 14-day total. All three models agree on the total within each window.

The aggregate files do not explain the difference between windows. Consequently, this comparison cannot establish that an identical set of orders was merely reallocated. The same-window percentages describe the reported results under each setting.

Together, these results distinguish three aspects of the performance picture: the direction of a model comparison, the channel’s relative position, and the sensitivity of the result to the lookback window. They provide the basis for the interpretation that follows.

Chapter 03 / Interpretation

What this study suggests for assessing channel performance.

Within this dataset, reported channel performance depends partly on the attribution settings used to describe it. The findings suggest three considerations when interpreting attributed order results.

Attribution settings qualify the performance comparison.

The Paid Search result shows that a statement such as “this channel receives more credit” is conditional on the chosen model. Algorithmic and linear attribution point in opposite directions relative to last-touch. This suggests that the metric, model, container, and lookback window form part of the evidence behind an assessment, because changing them can change the reported comparison.

Relative change and channel position describe different aspects of performance.

Email’s lower algorithmic credit does not remove its leading rank, while Direct Mail’s large percentage increase begins from a smaller baseline. These findings suggest that credit sensitivity is more informative when considered alongside attributed order volume and rank. A percentage increase or decrease alone does not describe the channel’s full position in this mix.

Consistency across settings supports a more qualified interpretation.

Direct Mail receives more credit under both alternatives at both windows, so the direction recurs across the settings examined. Its magnitude still varies. SMS shows how a window-dependent magnitude can also change a threshold-based classification. The study therefore suggests a distinction between observations that recur across settings and labels that depend on a particular window or boundary. Recurrence here describes consistency within these exports; it does not establish causal contribution.

The scope of this interpretation

The study compares aggregate outputs from one month of demo data. Alternative models provide comparison points, but the exports do not establish which allocation best represents a channel’s incremental effect on orders.

Reported revenue, conversion environments, and lifecycle metrics provide additional context, shown below. Their incomplete definitions limit further interpretation. Journey-level records and reporting configuration would be needed to examine the causes of the allocation differences; cost, margin, and experimental evidence would be needed to assess economic or incremental performance.

Supporting analysis: conversion environments, revenue, and lifecycle metrics

These measures describe the channel mix alongside the attribution results. They do not determine which attribution allocation is more accurate.

Where attributed orders were completed

“Paid Search” describes a marketing channel. “Call Center” describes where an order was completed. In the last-touch, 30-day breakdown, Call Center has the largest share for 14 of 16 channels.

For Email, Website is the largest environment at 31.08%, although it is not a majority. For In-App Messaging, Mobile App accounts for 58.64%. These patterns add operational context to the attribution comparison.

Revenue context

Revenue per attributed order

Reported revenue divided by last-touch attributed orders provides another view of the channel mix. For example, the derived values are 909.44 for Paid Search and 826.63 for Email, in the export’s unspecified revenue units.

These figures are not ROAS or profit. Advertising costs, margins, and the revenue metric’s detailed attribution definition are not included in the exports.

Supporting analysis

Lifecycle context and credit change

Across 16 channels, the exported Lifecycle Index has a Pearson correlation of 0.547 and a Spearman correlation of 0.335 with the 30-day algorithmic credit change.

The positive association is descriptive and depends on the measure of association. It does not establish a channel’s role in a customer journey or explain why the model allocates credit differently.

Metric definition limit: the export labels Acquisition Rate, Closure Rate, and Lifecycle Index are retained. The underlying CJA definitions of the first two are not supplied. In the data, Lifecycle Index equals Acquisition Rate minus Closure Rate. This study does not treat those fields as verified customer conversion probabilities.

Returning to the research question

In these demo exports, using last-touch produces a different view of channel performance from the alternative models. The differences concern order credit, some rankings, and the size of the comparison across lookback windows. This study suggests that attribution settings and the consistency of results across them are relevant considerations when interpreting reported channel performance.
